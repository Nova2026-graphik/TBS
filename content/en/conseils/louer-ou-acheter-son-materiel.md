The question keeps coming from hotels, event venues and caterers as their
business grows: "with all this renting, wouldn't we be better off buying?" The
answer depends on three figures, and rarely on the one that comes to mind
first.

## The apparent calculation

Take two hundred banquet chairs.

- **Renting**: a price per chair per event.
- **Buying**: a unit price, once.

Divide the purchase by the rental and you get the number of events from which
buying would pay off. On simple furniture, that threshold often falls between
**fifteen and twenty-five uses**.

That calculation decides most purchases. It is also the one that leaves out
what matters.

## The four costs the apparent calculation ignores

**Storage.** Two hundred chairs and twenty round tables take up some forty
square metres, under cover and dry. In a rented unit in Lomé, that is a monthly
cost that runs whether the equipment is used or not.

**Transport and handling.** Renting means buying delivery, set-up and
collection too. Owning means having a suitable vehicle, staff to load it, and
time.

**Upkeep and replacement.** A banquet chair degrades mainly in transport, not
in use. Count **10 to 15 % annual replacement** on furniture that moves
regularly, more on tableware and glassware. Bought stock is never a frozen
investment: it is a recurring cost line.

**Rigidity.** This is the heaviest cost, and the only one that cannot be put in
figures. A stock of two hundred chairs serves receptions of two hundred people
admirably. For five hundred, you rent the difference — often in a different
model, which shows in the room. For eighty, you have paid to store a hundred
and twenty unused chairs.

## The real calculation

The real break-even point, full costs included, sits closer to **forty to
sixty uses a year** for standard furniture — that is, sustained activity that
is also **steady in volume**.

Three profiles emerge:

**Buying makes sense** if you run more than one event a week, at comparable
volume, in a fixed venue — a hotel with its own function room, an event hall.
The equipment barely moves, wear is low, storage already exists.

**Renting makes sense** if your volumes vary, if your events take place
somewhere other than your own premises, or if you want to change style from one
reception to the next.

**A mix often makes the most sense**: own the core that is used every time —
the chairs of a fixed venue, a few tables — and rent everything that varies,
tableware first. That is the arrangement most of the professionals we work with
settle on.

## The special case of tableware

Tableware deserves its own reasoning. It breaks, it goes mismatched, and it
needs washing — washing to standard, with drying and inspection before storage.

A full range for three hundred covers loses, in our experience, a tenth of its
pieces a year. Replacing it identically assumes the model is still being
imported, which is never guaranteed over three years.

This is the item where renting keeps the advantage longest, even at high
volume.
