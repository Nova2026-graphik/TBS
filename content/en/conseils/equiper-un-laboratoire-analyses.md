An analysis laboratory is not judged by its most expensive analyser, but by its
ability to return a reliable result every day. This list follows the order in
which we equip: the items that block activity first, those that improve it
after.

## 1. Sampling and reception

The least costly item, and the one that decides the quality of everything else.
A poorly taken or poorly stored sample invalidates the most sophisticated
analysis.

- Phlebotomy chairs, tourniquets, tube racks;
- Vacuum tubes by analysis type, needles, safety systems;
- Storage refrigerator with a **temperature logger**;
- Labelling and an intake register.

The temperature logger is not optional: it is the component that lets you
prove, if ever challenged, that the cold chain held.

## 2. Centrifugation and preparation

- Bench centrifuge, capacity matched to daily throughput;
- Variable-volume micropipettes, with matching tips;
- Shaker, water bath, timer.

**The trade-off that counts**: the number of buckets on the centrifuge. An
undersized machine forces you to run two batches, which doubles preparation
time at peak hours — often between 7 and 10 a.m., when most patients arrive.

## 3. Analysis

This is the heaviest item, and the one where the choice commits you for years.

- **Biochemistry analyser** — the heart of day-to-day activity;
- **Haematology analyser** — full blood count;
- A good binocular microscope, with an immersion objective;
- Strip reader, coagulation instrument depending on your focus.

**The real cost is not the instrument, it is the reagents.** An analyser bought
at a good price but whose reagents are proprietary, imported and expensive will
cost more over two years than a competitor that is dearer to buy. Always ask
for the cost per test, reagents and calibrators included, and project it onto
your real volume.

Also check **local availability of after-sales service**. An analyser down for
three weeks waiting for a part means activity stopped.

## 4. Quality control

Often dealt with last, it should come first.

- Control sera, run every day;
- Calibrators, at the frequency the manufacturer prescribes;
- A control register and written procedures.

Without daily control, nothing distinguishes a correct result from a drifted
one — and an analyser's drift cannot be seen by eye.

## 5. Safety and disposal

- Fume hood or microbiological safety cabinet depending on the analyses
  performed;
- Sharps containers and a disposal route;
- Autoclave if bacteriology is performed;
- Personal protective equipment in sufficient quantity.

## 6. Computing and power

- Data entry station, report printer, management software;
- **A UPS for every analyser**;
- A generator or back-up solution.

In our climate, a power cut is not an exceptional event: it is a design
parameter. An analyser that stops mid-cycle loses the run in progress, and
sometimes its calibration.

## What weighs most on the budget

In decreasing order, over three years of operation:

1. **Reagents and consumables** — by far the largest item;
2. The analysers;
3. Maintenance and service contracts;
4. Furniture and fitting out;
5. Small instrumentation.

An equipment plan that reasons only about purchase price is therefore wrong
about its main item.
