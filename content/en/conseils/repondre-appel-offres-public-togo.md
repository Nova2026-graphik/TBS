A well-priced offer can be set aside without being read. Not out of ill will:
because an attestation is missing, because a document is more than three months
old, or because the technical and financial envelopes were not separated as the
tender required.

Here is what we check systematically before submitting.

## The administrative file

This is where most bidders lose. The documents required vary by contracting
authority, but the core is stable:

- **Trade register (RCCM)** — an up-to-date copy; the company name must match
  the one on the offer character for character;
- **Tax identification number (NIF)**;
- **Certificate of non-bankruptcy**;
- **Tax clearance certificate**, currently valid;
- **CNSS certificate**, proving social contributions are up to date;
- **Criminal record extract** for the manager, often required to be less than
  three months old;
- **Articles of association** and identity document of the signatory.

**Validity dates are the leading ground for rejection.** A tax certificate
issued four months ago for a file that requires three counts as a missing
document. Keep these documents together in a standing file and **renew them
every quarter**, independently of any ongoing tender: the submission deadline
is rarely compatible with the time it takes to obtain a certificate.

## The technical offer

It demonstrates that you know how to do the work, not that you are cheaper.

**Verifiable references** carry weight. A useful reference gives the client's
name, the nature of the supply, the amount and the year — and it can be
confirmed by a contact. Three precise references are worth more than fifteen
unverifiable lines.

**Technical datasheets** for the products offered must match the specification
point by point. If a specification cannot be met exactly, it is better to offer
an equivalent **and say so** than to leave it out: a reasoned equivalence gets
examined, a discrepancy discovered at opening eliminates you.

**Resources**: staff assigned, equipment available, storage capacity, committed
delivery times. This is where a local supplier gains the advantage over a
distant importer.

## The financial offer

A schedule of unit prices, a summary, and above all: **the same structure as
the one requested**. Renumbering the lots or merging two lines is enough to
make offers incomparable — and an incomparable offer is an offer set aside.

Check that the amount in figures matches the amount in words, the VAT, and the
total carried over to the submission form. A discrepancy between two pages of
the same file forces the committee to decide, and it rarely decides in your
favour.

## The form of the submission

The tender specifies the number of copies — often one original and two copies
— the binding, and the separation of envelopes. **These rules are not
formalities**: they guarantee that the price stays anonymous until the
technical opening, and a badly assembled envelope compromises the whole
procedure.

Submit the day before. On the day itself, a traffic jam in Lomé costs you a
contract.

## Delivery times, committed

A stated lead time is binding. For equipment in stock, we state 48 hours. For
equipment to be imported, two to six weeks depending on the nature of the
goods — and that period runs from notification, not from the order.

Announcing three weeks for equipment that takes six is the surest way to win a
contract and lose a client.
