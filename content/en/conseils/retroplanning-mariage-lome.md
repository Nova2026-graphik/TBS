A wedding is prepared over months, but the equipment is only really settled in
the final eight weeks. Too early and the numbers are still moving; too late and
the stock is gone. Here is the schedule we recommend to families, and what is
at stake at each step.

## W-8: lock the date

It is the only thing that matters at this stage. In the dry season — November
to March — Saturdays are booked two to four weeks ahead, and the large
marquees go first.

An order of magnitude is enough to book: "around six hundred guests, seated
dinner". Exact quantities come later, and they can move by 15 % without the
booking having to be redone.

**What gets decided**: the date, the venue, the order of magnitude.
**What can wait**: everything else.

## W-6: settle the format

Seated dinner, cocktail, or both? This decision drives everything else — the
number of chairs, the floor area, the tableware, right down to the type of
sound system.

It is also the moment to settle the outdoor question. A reception under a
marquee is not sized like one indoors: you have to plan for the ground, the
lighting, and a fallback if rain arrives out of season.

## W-4: visit the warehouse

Colours cannot be chosen on a screen. The ivory of a catalogue and the ivory of
a tablecloth under warm lighting are not the same colour, and the difference
shows in the photographs.

Our warehouses in Agôè-Démakpoè can be visited Monday to Saturday by
appointment. Allow an hour: you see the chairs, the tableware ranges and the
linen for real, and you leave with a decision rather than an intention.

## W-3: freeze the quantities

This is the week the guest count becomes serious. Replies are in, the list is
stable to within a few names.

Now go back to the ratios: one seat per guest plus 5 %, one table per ten,
2.5 plates per diner. A firm quote comes out of that, and it does not move
again — unless you add something.

**The classic trap**: leaving children out of the count. They occupy a chair,
use a plate and break glasses like everyone else.

## W-2: the floor plan

The floor plan is not decoration: it determines where the waiting staff pass,
where the sound system sits, where the dance floor opens.

A service aisle of one metre twenty between two rows of tables is not a luxury
— it is what makes it possible to serve six hundred hot covers. We draw it with
you from the venue's real dimensions.

## W-1: confirm the access

The question everyone forgets: **where does the truck come in?**

A gate that is too narrow, a sloping yard, an upper floor with no goods lift —
each of these details adds two hours to the set-up, and those two hours come
out of the night before the ceremony. A twenty-minute site visit avoids them.

## The day itself: set up before everyone else

Set-up finishes **before the first guest arrives**, without exception. That
means starting early: for a reception at 6 p.m., the team is on site at
10 a.m.

Whatever is left for the final hour — fresh flowers, menus, guest favours —
is prepared while the bulk is already in place.

## Afterwards: the take-down

Take-down happens the next day, not the same evening. Nobody wants to count
glasses at two in the morning, and that is how things get broken.

Return the equipment cleared but unwashed: washing is part of the service, and
every piece goes back out checked for the next hire.

---

This schedule holds for a wedding of three to seven hundred guests. Below that,
everything compresses without harm. Above it, add two weeks: it is not the
equipment that runs short, it is the time to set it up.
